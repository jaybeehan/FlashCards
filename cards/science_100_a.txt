## Science 9 | Biological Diversity
What is biodiversity? :: The variety of life in an ecosystem, including species, genetic and ecosystem diversity.
What are the three levels of biodiversity? :: Genetic diversity, species diversity and ecosystem diversity.
What is genetic diversity? :: The variety of genes within a species.
What is species diversity? :: The number and abundance of different species in an area.
What is ecosystem diversity? :: The variety of habitats and ecosystems in a region.
Why is biodiversity important? :: It keeps ecosystems stable and provides food, medicine and materials.
What is a species? :: A group of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring.
What is a population? :: All the members of one species in an area.
What is a community? :: All the populations living in an area.
What is an ecosystem? :: A community together with its non-living environment.
What is a habitat? :: The place where an organism lives.
What is DNA? :: The molecule that carries the genetic instructions of an organism.
What is a gene? :: A segment of DNA that codes for a trait.
What is a chromosome? :: A coiled structure of DNA and protein carrying many genes.
How many chromosomes are in a human body cell? :: 46 (23 pairs).
What is an allele? :: A form of a gene.
What is a dominant allele? :: An allele that shows its trait when at least one copy is present.
What is a recessive allele? :: An allele that shows only when two copies are present.
What is a genotype? :: The set of alleles an organism has.
What is a phenotype? :: The visible traits of an organism.
What does homozygous mean? :: Two identical alleles for a gene.
What does heterozygous mean? :: Two different alleles for a gene.
What is a Punnett square used for? :: Predicting the possible genotypes of offspring.
What is the genotype ratio from Bb x Bb? :: 1 BB : 2 Bb : 1 bb
What is the phenotype ratio from Bb x Bb? :: 3 dominant : 1 recessive
What is the probability of a bb offspring from Bb x Bb? :: 1/4
What are the offspring of BB x bb? :: All Bb.
What are the offspring of Bb x bb? :: 1/2 Bb and 1/2 bb.
What is a mutation? :: A change in DNA that may change a trait.
Can mutations be helpful? :: Yes, harmful, neutral or helpful.
What is asexual reproduction? :: Reproduction by one parent producing identical offspring.
Name two types of asexual reproduction. :: Binary fission and budding.
What is budding? :: A new organism grows as an outgrowth of the parent, like in yeast or hydra.
What is binary fission? :: A cell splits into two identical cells.
What is sexual reproduction? :: Reproduction involving two parents and the mixing of genes.
Why does sexual reproduction increase variation? :: Offspring get a new combination of genes from two parents.
What is a clone? :: A genetically identical copy of an organism.
What is natural selection? :: Organisms with advantageous traits survive and reproduce more.
Who proposed natural selection? :: Charles Darwin.
What is an adaptation? :: A trait that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its environment.
Give an example of a structural adaptation. :: A cactus's spines or a polar bear's thick fur.
Give an example of a behavioural adaptation. :: Migration or hibernation.
Give an example of a physiological adaptation. :: Antifreeze proteins in Arctic fish.
What is camouflage? :: Colouring or shape that helps an organism blend into its surroundings.
What is mimicry? :: One species resembling another to gain protection.
What is the peppered moth example? :: Dark moths became common in polluted areas because they were better camouflaged.
What is selective breeding? :: Humans choose parents with desired traits to produce offspring.
Name an example of selective breeding. :: Dog breeds, canola, dairy cattle.
What is genetic engineering? :: Directly changing an organism's DNA.
What is a GMO? :: A genetically modified organism.
Name a benefit of GMO crops. :: Higher yield, pest or herbicide resistance.
Name a concern about GMO crops. :: Possible environmental or health effects and corporate control.
What is cloning's example? :: Dolly the sheep, cloned in 1996.
What is extinction? :: The complete loss of a species.
What is an endangered species? :: A species at risk of extinction.
What is a threatened species? :: A species likely to become endangered.
What is an extirpated species? :: A species that has disappeared from a region but still exists elsewhere.
Name an endangered species in Canada. :: Whooping crane, swift fox or northern spotted owl.
What is habitat loss? :: The destruction or fragmentation of natural habitat.
What are invasive species? :: Non-native species that spread and harm ecosystems.
What is the Species at Risk Act? :: A Canadian law to protect endangered and threatened wildlife.
What is COSEWIC? :: The Committee on the Status of Endangered Wildlife in Canada.
What is a seed bank? :: A facility storing seeds to preserve plant diversity.
What is a zoo's conservation role? :: Breeding programs and education for endangered animals.
How does biodiversity help ecosystems recover? :: More species mean more ways to respond to change.
What is a monoculture? :: A crop of a single variety grown over a large area.
Why are monocultures risky? :: One disease can destroy the whole crop.
What is binomial nomenclature? :: A two-part scientific naming system: genus and species.
What is the scientific name of humans? :: Homo sapiens
Name the levels of classification from broadest to narrowest. :: Domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.

What is a pollutant? :: A substance that harms the environment.

What is runoff? :: Water flowing over land, carrying pollutants.

What are heavy metals? :: Dense metals like lead and mercury that are toxic.
What is groundwater contamination? :: Pollutants seeping into underground water supplies.

What is hard water? :: Water with high dissolved calcium and magnesium.

What is a heat engine? :: A device that turns thermal energy into mechanical work.
What is a thermal conductor? :: A material that transfers heat easily.
What is a thermal insulator? :: A material that resists heat transfer.
What is conduction? :: Heat transfer by direct contact.
What is convection? :: Heat transfer by the movement of fluids.
What is radiation? :: Heat transfer by electromagnetic waves.
What is specific heat capacity? :: Energy needed to raise 1 g of a substance by 1 degree C.
What is the specific heat of water? :: 4.19 J/(g C)
Find the energy to heat 200 g of water by 10 degrees C. :: 8380 J
What is a renewable energy source? :: One that replenishes naturally, like wind or solar.
What is a non-renewable energy source? :: One that is used up faster than it forms, like oil.
Name a way to improve a device's efficiency. :: Reduce friction and heat loss.
What is regenerative braking? :: Converting braking energy back into electrical energy.
What is a hybrid vehicle? :: A vehicle using both a gasoline engine and electric motor.
What is cogeneration? :: Producing electricity and useful heat from one fuel.
What is energy conservation? :: Using less energy.

## Science 10 | Cycling of Matter in Living Systems
What is photosynthesis? :: Plants use light, CO2 and water to make glucose and oxygen.
What is the equation for photosynthesis? :: 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2
What is cellular respiration? :: Cells break down glucose with oxygen to release energy.
What is the equation for cellular respiration? :: C6H12O6 + 6 O2 -> 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + energy
Where does photosynthesis occur? :: In chloroplasts.
Where does cellular respiration occur? :: Mostly in mitochondria.
What is chlorophyll? :: The green pigment that absorbs light for photosynthesis.
What are stomata? :: Pores in leaves for gas exchange.
What is ATP? :: The cell's energy currency.
What is a producer? :: An organism that makes its own food.
What is a consumer? :: An organism that eats other organisms.
What is a herbivore? :: An animal that eats plants.
What is a carnivore? :: An animal that eats other animals.
What is an omnivore? :: An animal that eats both plants and animals.
What is a decomposer? :: An organism that breaks down dead material.
Name a decomposer. :: Fungi or bacteria.
What is a detritivore? :: An organism that feeds on dead organic matter, like earthworms.
What is a food chain? :: A single path showing who eats whom.
What is a food web? :: Many interconnected food chains.
What is a trophic level? :: A feeding level in a food chain.
What is the 10% rule? :: About 10% of energy passes to the next trophic level.
Why are food chains short? :: Energy is lost at each level.
What is an energy pyramid? :: A diagram showing energy at each trophic level.
What is a biomass pyramid? :: A diagram of the total mass at each trophic level.
What is the carbon cycle? :: The movement of carbon among air, water, organisms and soil.
How does carbon enter living things? :: Through photosynthesis.
How does carbon return to the atmosphere? :: Respiration, decomposition and burning.
What are carbon sinks? :: Forests, oceans and soils that store carbon.
What is the nitrogen cycle? :: The movement of nitrogen among air, soil and organisms.
What is nitrogen fixation? :: Converting N2 to forms plants can use.
What organisms fix nitrogen? :: Certain bacteria, often in legume root nodules.
What is nitrification? :: Bacteria converting ammonium to nitrate.
What is denitrification? :: Bacteria returning nitrogen to the air.
What is the phosphorus cycle? :: The movement of phosphorus through rocks, soil, water and organisms.
What is the water cycle? :: Evaporation, condensation, precipitation and collection.
What is transpiration? :: Water vapour released from plant leaves.
What is a biome? :: A large region defined by climate and its living things.
Name three Canadian biomes. :: Boreal forest, tundra, grassland.
What is a biotic factor? :: A living part of an ecosystem.
What is an abiotic factor? :: A non-living part of an ecosystem.
What is a limiting factor? :: A resource that limits population growth.
What is carrying capacity? :: The largest population an environment can support.
What is a population? :: All members of a species in an area.
What is population density? :: The number of individuals per unit area.
What is symbiosis? :: A close relationship between two species.
What is mutualism? :: Both species benefit.
What is parasitism? :: One benefits and the other is harmed.
What is commensalism? :: One benefits and the other is unaffected.
What is competition? :: Organisms struggle for the same resources.
What is predation? :: One organism hunts another for food.
What is succession? :: A gradual change in the community of an area.
What is bioaccumulation? :: Build-up of a toxin in an organism.
What is biomagnification? :: Increase of a toxin up the food chain.
What is an invasive species? :: A non-native species that harms an ecosystem.
Give an invasive species found in Alberta. :: Leafy spurge, Canada thistle or Norway rats.
What is a keystone species? :: A species with a large effect on its ecosystem.
What is deforestation's effect on carbon? :: Less CO2 absorption and more CO2 released.
What is an ecological footprint? :: The land and resources needed to support a person or population.
What is sustainability? :: Meeting needs without harming future generations.

## Science 10 | Energy Flow in Global Systems
What is weather? :: Short-term atmospheric conditions.
What is climate? :: The average weather over a long period.
What drives weather? :: Energy from the Sun and Earth's rotation.
What is the atmosphere? :: The layer of gases around Earth.
What are the layers of the atmosphere? :: Troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere, exosphere.
Where does weather occur? :: In the troposphere.
What is the ozone layer's location? :: In the stratosphere.
What is air pressure? :: The force of air molecules on a surface.
What is a high-pressure system? :: Sinking air bringing clear, dry weather.
What is a low-pressure system? :: Rising air bringing clouds and precipitation.
What is wind? :: Air moving from high to low pressure.
What is the Coriolis effect? :: The curving of winds and currents by Earth's rotation.
What is a jet stream? :: A fast current of high-altitude air.
What is humidity? :: The amount of water vapour in the air.
What is relative humidity? :: The percent of water vapour the air holds compared to its maximum.
What is the dew point? :: The temperature at which air becomes saturated and dew forms.
What is a front? :: The boundary between two air masses.
What happens at a cold front? :: Cold air pushes under warm air, often bringing storms.
What happens at a warm front? :: Warm air rises over cold air, bringing steady rain.
What is a chinook? :: A warm, dry wind that descends the east side of the Rockies.
What is a rain shadow? :: A dry region on the leeward side of mountains.
What is a thunderstorm? :: A storm with lightning, thunder and heavy rain.
What is a tornado? :: A rotating column of air touching the ground.
What is a hurricane? :: A large tropical storm with strong winds.
What is the Fujita scale? :: A scale rating tornado strength.
What is an ocean current? :: A large flow of ocean water.
What is the Gulf Stream? :: A warm Atlantic current that warms northwest Europe.
What is thermohaline circulation? :: Global ocean circulation driven by temperature and salt differences.
What is El Nino? :: A periodic warming of Pacific waters that changes global weather.
What is La Nina? :: A periodic cooling of Pacific waters.
Why is Edmonton colder than Vancouver in winter? :: Vancouver is warmed by the ocean; Edmonton is inland.
What is specific heat capacity? :: Energy needed to raise 1 g of a substance by 1 degree C.
Why does water moderate climates? :: It heats and cools slowly because of its high specific heat.
What is albedo? :: The fraction of sunlight a surface reflects.
Which has higher albedo: snow or forest? :: Snow.
What is the greenhouse effect? :: Gases trap heat in the atmosphere.
What are greenhouse gases? :: CO2, CH4, N2O and water vapour.
What is global warming? :: A long-term rise in Earth's average temperature.
What is climate change? :: A long-term change in global or regional climate patterns.
What are signs of climate change? :: Rising temperatures, melting ice, sea level rise and more extreme weather.
What is a carbon sink? :: A system that absorbs more carbon than it releases.
What is a carbon source? :: A system that releases more carbon than it absorbs.
What is the Paris Agreement? :: A 2015 deal to limit warming well below 2 degrees C.
What is a carbon tax? :: A fee on carbon emissions.
What is renewable energy? :: Energy from naturally replenished sources.
What are examples of renewable energy? :: Solar, wind, hydro, geothermal and biomass.
What is the Sun's role in the water cycle? :: It provides energy for evaporation.
What is evaporation? :: Liquid turning to gas below boiling point.
What is condensation? :: Gas turning to liquid.
What is precipitation? :: Water falling from clouds as rain, snow, sleet or hail.
What is a glacier? :: A large mass of ice that moves slowly.
What is permafrost? :: Ground that stays frozen for years.
Why is melting permafrost a problem? :: It releases greenhouse gases and damages buildings.
What is sea level rise caused by? :: Melting land ice and thermal expansion of water.
What is thermal expansion? :: Water expanding as it warms.
What is a drought? :: A long period of unusually low precipitation.
What is a flood? :: Water overflowing onto normally dry land.
What is a wildfire's link to climate? :: Warmer, drier conditions increase wildfire risk.
What is a heat dome? :: A persistent high-pressure system trapping hot air.
What is a climate model? :: A computer simulation predicting climate change.
How do scientists study past climates? :: Ice cores, tree rings and sediment layers.
What does an ice core show? :: Past atmospheric gases and temperatures.
What are solar flares' effects? :: They can disrupt satellites and power grids.
What is the aurora? :: Light in the sky caused by charged particles hitting the atmosphere.
